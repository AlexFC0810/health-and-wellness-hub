# Testosterone, fertility preservation & peptide decision map

_Last researched: 2026-10-08. Evidence review; not a personal medical record, diagnosis, or treatment protocol._

## Decision thesis
**Do not equate "optimization" with evidence of a deficiency.** At any adult age, assess symptoms, obtain reliable repeat measurements, investigate potentially reversible causes, establish fertility priorities, and choose the least-disruptive effective intervention. Do not start a suppressive lifelong-leaning therapy solely because a person expects to try it eventually.

## TRT: what is and is not reversible
- External testosterone suppresses hypothalamic-pituitary signaling (LH/FSH) and often sperm production. Some men recover endogenous production after stopping; recovery and fertility may take months or longer and are not assured.
- "Just once per month" with conventional short-acting injections does **not** create a reliable non-suppressive strategy. Peaks and troughs can worsen symptom stability while natural production may still be suppressed.
- Clinically indicated TRT can alleviate symptoms of confirmed hypogonadism. It requires monitoring (symptoms, testosterone, hematocrit, blood pressure, relevant contraindications and risk assessment); not an established cognitive or business-performance enhancer for men with normal testosterone.
- FDA testosterone labeling evolved in 2025–2026, including TRAVERSE cardiovascular results and blood-pressure risk. Those trials and label changes do not make non-indicated TRT harmless, particularly in young men.

## Fertility-aware options (specialist-directed, not DIY)
| Option | Mechanism/intent | Evidence and limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Address sleep, body composition, excessive training stress, medication/illness factors | Preserve or restore endogenous function | Low-disruption first step where causal contributors exist; benefit depends on actual cause. |
| Clomiphene or enclomiphene (SERMs) | Increase pituitary LH/FSH if axis is responsive | Possible in selected hypogonadal men; off-label or not FDA-approved as a male testosterone drug; not appropriate for every pattern of hypogonadism; adverse effects and monitoring matter. |
| hCG | Acts like LH to stimulate testicular testosterone | Established role in selected reproductive endocrinology cases; medically supervised, not guaranteed fertility insurance. |
| Exogenous TRT (gel/injection/etc.) | Replaces testosterone | Can impair spermatogenesis; especially important when future fatherhood is a priority. |

## Diagnostic pathway
1. Specify symptoms: libido, erections, morning erections, fatigue, strength/recovery, mood, sleep quality and any sleep apnea concerns. Fatigue/poor recovery are nonspecific.
2. With a clinician, obtain **two separate fasting total testosterone tests after a regular sleep period**, timed appropriately for the person's sleep-work schedule. Use reliable laboratory assays; don't act on one outlier. A common decision threshold is around 300 ng/dL, interpreted with symptoms, assay, and clinical context.
3. If low or borderline, consider SHBG and calculated/measured free testosterone when indicated; LH and FSH to distinguish primary vs secondary dysfunction, with further targeted tests (e.g., prolactin when indicated). Other labs depend on symptoms and clinical review: CBC, metabolic/thyroid assessments, HbA1c, lipids, and blood pressure.
4. Document near-/long-term fertility goals before therapy. Discuss semen analysis / fertility preservation if there is meaningful potential for future biological children.
5. Address reversible causes first where appropriate; re-test; if confirmed medical hypogonadism persists, compare benefit, risks, reversibility, time burden and cost with clinician.
6. If medicated, use a defined objective, outcome assessment and adverse-effect monitoring plan, rather than a target high-end testosterone number.

## Peptide categorization
- **FDA-approved peptide medications** exist and have product-specific indications and safety evidence (not equivalent to a broad endorsement of "peptide therapy").
- **Unapproved/compounded experimental peptides** like BPC-157, CJC-1295, ipamorelin and similar products have inconsistent or limited controlled human safety/efficacy evidence; FDA has published specific risks. Compounded medications are not FDA-approved, even if produced in licensed or regulated settings.
- Regulatory proposals and compounding availability can change; **availability is not proof of efficacy or safety**.
- Require exact compound, claimed indication, human RCT evidence, adverse-event data, supplier and pharmacy verification, and transparent informed consent; never infer safety from marketing testimonials.

## Questions to ask a provider
- Will you require symptoms **and two separate low morning total testosterone tests** before considering TRT?
- How do you measure/interpret LH, FSH, SHBG/free T, sleep and alternative causes?
- If fertility matters, what options come *before* exogenous testosterone? What is the discontinuation/recovery plan?
- What is the intended therapeutic target, and how will treatment change if symptoms don't improve?
- Which product is FDA-approved vs compounded/off-label? Name of dispensing pharmacy, clinical supervisor, credentials, cost of all labs/follow-ups, and adverse-event procedures?
- For peptide proposals: exact compound, FDA status, human data supporting this indication, and unknowns?
