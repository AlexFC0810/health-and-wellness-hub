# Evidence Sovereignty Protocol

## Purpose

Health decisions should not be outsourced to institutional prestige, commercial marketing, cultural tradition, mechanistic storytelling, or individual testimony. Every source is evidence submitted for cross-examination.

This protocol is designed to identify real signal while resisting:

- pharmaceutical and device-industry capture;
- regulatory and professional inertia;
- publication and funding bias;
- alternative-health marketing;
- charismatic-authority effects;
- survivorship and testimonial bias;
- mechanistic overreach;
- geographic or ideological tribalism;
- our own preferred conclusions.

## The evidence stack

No layer is sovereign. Confidence rises when independent layers converge.

1. **First-principles physiology** — causal pathway, biological compatibility, dose, selectivity, reversibility.
2. **Traditional and historical use** — indication, preparation, dose, duration, cultural persistence, recorded harms.
3. **Distributed lived experience** — repeated independent reports, practitioner pattern recognition, community experience.
4. **Documented case evidence** — confirmed diagnosis, complete protocol, timeline, objective outcomes, follow-up.
5. **Mechanistic evidence** — receptors, enzymes, metabolism, immune pathways, pharmacokinetics.
6. **Cell and tissue evidence** — useful for discovery; weak for direct clinical claims.
7. **Animal evidence** — useful for whole-organism effects; translation remains uncertain.
8. **Observational human evidence** — real-world association, vulnerable to confounding.
9. **Controlled human evidence** — estimates causal effects under defined conditions.
10. **Independent replication** — reduces sponsor, laboratory, and publication-specific error.
11. **Long-term and real-world outcomes** — survival, function, quality of life, recurrence, adherence, delayed harms.
12. **Systems evidence** — interactions among diet, sleep, environment, movement, treatment, and social context.

## Distributed human experience

Experience is not “nothing.” It is often the discovery engine that precedes formal research. It becomes stronger when:

- reports arise independently across cultures or locations;
- the indication and preparation are consistent;
- objective changes accompany subjective improvement;
- timing is reproducible;
- withdrawal and reintroduction produce consistent effects;
- nonresponders and adverse events are counted;
- the denominator is known;
- long-term follow-up exists.

### Testimonial audit

For every remarkable account, ask:

- Was the diagnosis confirmed?
- What was the baseline severity?
- What else changed at the same time?
- Did conventional treatment occur before or during the intervention?
- How many people tried the protocol?
- How many improved, failed, worsened, or disappeared from follow-up?
- Was the outcome objective and durable?
- Is the storyteller selling the intervention?
- What competing explanation best fits the timeline?

A testimonial without a denominator is a hypothesis, not a cure rate.

## Claim decomposition

Never evaluate a broad claim such as “X helps cancer” or “parasites cause poor health.” Rewrite it into:

- Which condition or organism?
- Which phenotype, stage, or severity?
- Which preparation and dose?
- Which population?
- Used alone or as an adjunct?
- What outcome?
- Over what time horizon?
- Compared with what complete alternative strategy?

## Evidence-state labels

Every repository claim should carry one label:

- **Established** — repeatedly demonstrated with meaningful human outcomes.
- **Strongly supported** — convergent human evidence with limited remaining uncertainty.
- **Promising** — credible early human signal or strong convergence short of validation.
- **Mechanistically plausible** — biological rationale exists; clinical benefit unknown.
- **Traditional / experiential signal** — persistent or repeated use worth investigating.
- **Anecdotally interesting** — potentially important but denominator and causality unclear.
- **Contradicted** — better evidence consistently fails to support the claim.
- **Unknown / under-researched** — evidence is insufficient, inaccessible, or structurally unfunded.

## Incentives audit

Apply symmetrically to every source:

- Who funded the work?
- Who designed the protocol and comparator?
- Who owns the data?
- Who performed the analysis and wrote the paper?
- Is the product patentable or proprietary?
- Does the author sell the treatment, supplement, clinic, book, or course?
- Are negative outcomes and withdrawals visible?
- Was the study preregistered?
- Were outcomes switched or selectively reported?
- Is absolute benefit reported, or only relative benefit?
- Who gains or loses if the conclusion is adopted?

## Geography and source diversity

Research should deliberately include multiple medical and cultural ecosystems:

- Europe and the UK;
- the Middle East;
- East and South Asia;
- Africa;
- Latin America;
- North America;
- traditional and indigenous knowledge where responsibly documented.

Geographic diversity is an anti-capture tool, not a truth guarantee. Every source receives the same methodological inspection.

## First-principles interrogation

For every intervention ask:

1. **Root cause:** Is this treating a driver, mechanism, symptom, or appearance?
2. **Biological distance:** Does it restore normal physiology, mimic it, modulate it, or override it?
3. **Dose:** Can an effective concentration reach the target safely?
4. **Selectivity:** Why should it affect the unwanted organism or cell more than healthy tissue?
5. **Compensation:** Can the system route around the blocked pathway?
6. **Exposure footprint:** Local or systemic? Brief or chronic? Reversible or persistent?
7. **Interaction load:** What happens when combined with diet, fasting, medication, supplements, or illness?
8. **Opportunity cost:** What happens if it fails or delays something time-sensitive?
9. **Falsifiability:** What result would make us downgrade or reject the hypothesis?
10. **Total outcome:** Did people live longer, function better, feel better, or sustain remission—not merely move a biomarker?

## Synergy without confusion

Chronic disease may respond to coordinated restoration rather than a single molecule. Combination protocols are valid research objects, but they must be structured.

Recommended method:

1. Establish baseline measurements.
2. Remove obvious harms first.
3. Stabilize foundational variables.
4. Introduce one intervention at a time when practical.
5. When testing a bundle, document every component and rationale.
6. Track benefits, harms, adherence, and timing.
7. Remove nonessential components after stabilization to identify the minimum effective system.
8. Publish failures and null results internally.

## Risk classes

### Class A — low-risk foundations

Sleep regularity, ordinary whole-food nutrition, walking, appropriate strength training, basic stress reduction, removal of irritating products.

### Class B — bounded self-experiments

Dietary trigger trials, carefully selected topical products, time-restricted eating in suitable adults, low-risk supplements with clear dosing and interaction review.

### Class C — clinically supervised interventions

Extended fasting, aggressive elimination diets, prescription medications, high-dose supplementation, treatment of suspected infections, hormone manipulation.

### Class D — time-sensitive or high-consequence decisions

Cancer, severe infection, organ dysfunction, neurological decline, pregnancy-related risk, immunosuppression, major surgery, rapidly worsening symptoms.

Class D decisions require explicit opportunity-cost analysis and should never rely on internet testimony alone.

## Research-output template

Every module should include:

- precise question;
- current evidence state;
- first-principles model;
- international evidence map;
- traditional and experiential signal;
- incentive/conflict audit;
- benefits and harms;
- phenotype or context where it may apply;
- practical decision tree;
- falsification criteria;
- unresolved questions;
- next experiment or research action.
